**Setup:** before the lesson, cut the foil into squares of the same size (three per group, one per round, plus spares), put about 60 pebbles in a container for each tub, and set shallow tubs of water on towels, one tub for every two groups. Groups of three work well: a builder, a placer and a counter. Swap jobs each round, so everyone does every job once. Draw the results table on the board (below).

**Focus:** engineers predict before they test, then test fairly so the result means something, then use the result to improve. The habit this week is **predict, then test**.

**No writing today:** every prediction, result and idea is said aloud or shown with a signal (opportunities to respond, PCMS Practice 6). Only the teacher writes, on the board.

**Links:** in Lessons 1 to 3 students built and improved LEGO designs, and they built paper bridges that held pebbles.

## How the challenge works

- **The problem:** build a boat that floats and holds as many pebbles as possible.
- **The constraints:** one square of foil per boat and no tape or other materials. Each round has 1 minute to plan and 4 minutes to build, on the timer in the slides.
- **Plan (1 minute, talk only):** hands off the foil. The group agrees on a shape and a prediction, and everyone in the group can say the number.
- **Build (4 minutes):** the timer chimes when planning ends and again when time is up. Hands off when it chimes.
- **Fair test:** at the tub, the group says its prediction aloud to the other group. Put the boat gently on the water. The placer adds pebbles one at a time in the middle of the boat while both groups count aloud. The score is the last pebble before water comes in or the boat sinks. The counter tells the teacher the score for the board.
- **Three rounds:** a fresh square each round. Between rounds, the design process slide asks "What happened? What will you change?"

**Key words:** constraint, fair test, float, sink, design, improve

## How students respond

Teach these before round 1 (pre-correction), and give 3 to 5 seconds of think time before every signal: "Think... 3, 2, 1, show me."

- **Fingers:** a number from 1 to 10. More than 10: both hands on your head, and the teacher asks for the number. Also 1, 2 or 3 fingers for A, B or C.
- **Thumbs:** up for yes or more, down for no or less, sideways for the same or not sure.
- **Choral:** "My turn, your turn" for new words, and the whole class says a one-answer response together on a signal.
- **Turn and talk:** 20 to 30 seconds with your group, then the teacher pulls a name stick for an answer. The group helps, so nobody is stuck.
- **Show:** hold up your boat, or stand up if a statement is true for your group.

## Before round 3: what makes a winning boat?

The aim is for students to find the pattern in their own results first, then see why it works, so round 3 is set up for success.

1. **Look at the board.** "Which boats held the most pebbles? What is the same about them?" Turn and talk, then name sticks. Expect ideas like "flat", "big bottom", "low sides". Write two or three on the board in the students' words.
2. **Predict.** Step 1 of the animation shows three boats from the same square: a tall ship, a boat folded in half and a wide flat boat. "Which will hold the most? Think... show me: 1, 2 or 3 fingers for A, B or C." Move on only after every hand is up.
3. **The tall ship** (old wooden ship shape): most of the foil goes into tall pointed ends and curved sides, so the base is narrow. It sits deep, and water comes in over the low middle first. The class counts the pebbles aloud with the animation.
4. **Folded in half:** two layers make it strong, but the base is half the size. The pebbles pile up high, and it tips.
5. **Wide and flat:** a tray with short sides. Boats like this are called punts. The pebbles spread out in one layer and it sinks slowly. This one usually wins. "Thumbs up if you picked C."
6. **Why:** a boat floats by pushing water out of the way, and the water pushes back up. A wide base pushes away a lot of water while sinking only a little, so it can carry more pebbles before water reaches the edge. Choral: "A wide base pushes away more water."
7. **Plan round 3:** wide, flat base; sides just high enough to keep the water out (short sides leave more foil for the base); pinch the corners tight so there are no gaps.

Sentence stems (said, not written): **We will change ___ because ___.** (between rounds) and **Our best boat held ___ pebbles because ___.** (share)

## Questions to ask while roaming

- What is your prediction? Why do you think that?
- Is your test fair? What are you keeping the same each time?
- Where did the water come in first? What could you change there?
- Was your result more or less than your prediction? Why do you think that happened?
- What did you change from the last round? Did it help?

## Support and extension

- **Support:** give a group a boat shape to copy for round 1 (a flat base with the sides folded up), so the challenge is the test and the prediction. Accept a signal instead of a sentence.
- **Extension:** test one change at a time across the rounds. Does a wider base or higher sides make the bigger difference? Predict first, then use rounds 2 and 3 to find out.

**Key idea to finish on:** engineers predict before they test, and they test fairly so they can trust the result. Comparing the result with the prediction tells us what to change next.

## Notes for the teacher

- **The timer** is built into the slides (one per round) and is also on its own as `timer.html`. Right arrow starts it, the next right arrow moves on, a tap pauses it, and the left arrow resets it. It chimes when planning ends and when time is up; the chime needs the computer's sound on.
- **Name sticks** spread the questions across the class. Put a stick aside once it has been used so every student gets a turn over the lesson.
- **Water safety:** keep the tubs on towels on the floor or a low table, and wipe spills straight away so the floor is not slippery. An adult empties the tubs.
- **Pebbles:** use pebbles of a similar size so results can be compared. Marbles work too, but roll off tables, so keep them in containers.
- **Foil edges** can be sharp. Fold them over if a student is worried.
- The flat square in the hook usually holds very few pebbles before water comes over the edge. That is the point: the material is the same, the shape is different, just like the paper bridge.
- Students who fold the foil in half for strength usually find their boat is too small to hold many pebbles, and students who make an old ship shape usually find water comes in early. Let this happen in rounds 1 and 2: it gives the class something to compare before round 3.
